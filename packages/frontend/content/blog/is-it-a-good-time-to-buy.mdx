# Is It a Good Time to Buy a House? What the Data Actually Says in 2026

**"Is it a good time to buy a house?"** It is the most-searched real estate question on the internet, and it has been for years. The honest answer is always the same: *it depends entirely on where you are buying and what your financial situation looks like.* But that answer, while accurate, is not very helpful.

So let us do better. Instead of offering generic advice or hot takes, we are going to walk through what the data actually says about buying a home in 2026 — nationally and in specific metros. We will show you which markets favor buyers right now, which ones still favor sellers, and how to use data to make a decision that is right for your situation.

## The Short Answer

For most of the country, **2026 is a better time to buy than any point since 2019.** Not because prices have crashed — they mostly have not — but because the market dynamics have shifted meaningfully in the buyer's favor:

- **Inventory is up.** Active listings are 12-15% higher year-over-year nationally. More choices mean less competition and more negotiating power.
- **Sellers are more flexible.** The share of listings with price reductions is at its highest level since 2018 in many markets. Seller concessions — rate buydowns, closing cost credits, repair allowances — are increasingly common.
- **Bidding wars have receded.** Outside of a handful of extremely supply-constrained markets, the days of 20 offers on a property within 48 hours are largely over.
- **Mortgage rates have stabilized.** Rates in the 5.8%-6.4% range are not cheap, but they are predictable. The uncertainty of 2023-2024, when rates swung wildly, made planning nearly impossible. Stability helps buyers budget with confidence.

That said, **affordability remains historically stretched.** Monthly payments are still significantly higher than pre-pandemic levels for most markets. And price corrections have been modest and concentrated — if you are waiting for a 2008-style crash, the data does not support that expectation.

## What the Data Says: Key National Metrics

Let us look at the numbers that matter most when asking whether it is a good time to buy a house.

### Mortgage Rates and Monthly Payments

The 30-year fixed mortgage rate is hovering around 6.1% as of early 2026 — down from the 7.8% peak in late 2023 but well above the 2.8%-3.2% range that prevailed during the pandemic. For a $400,000 home with 20% down, that translates to roughly $1,940 per month in principal and interest, compared to $1,330 at 3%. That $610 monthly difference is the single biggest affordability headwind.

However, rates have been stable for several months, and most forecasters expect them to drift slightly lower through 2026 as the Federal Reserve continues its gradual easing. If you buy now and rates decline further, refinancing is always an option — you marry the house, you date the rate.

### Inventory and Months of Supply

National active inventory is at its highest level since mid-2020. Months of supply — the number of months it would take to sell all active listings at the current sales pace — sits at approximately 3.8 months nationally. A balanced market is typically considered 4-6 months, so we are close to equilibrium for the first time in years.

The important nuance: inventory growth is heavily concentrated in the Sun Belt and West. The Northeast and Midwest remain supply-constrained, with many markets still below 2 months of supply.

### Price-to-Income Ratio

The national median home price relative to median household income is approximately 4.8x — elevated by historical standards (the long-run average is closer to 3.5x) but improved from the 5.2x peak in early 2024. Markets below 3.5x are generally considered affordable; markets above 5x face structural demand constraints.

### Days on Market

Homes are sitting on the market longer than at any point since 2019 in most metros. National median days on market is around 42 days, up from 25 days at the 2022 trough. Longer DOM means less pressure to make hasty decisions and more room to negotiate.

## Markets Where It Is a Great Time to Buy

Based on PropertyIQ's analysis, these metros offer the most favorable conditions for buyers in 2026:

### Affordable Markets with Strong Fundamentals

**[Detroit-Warren-Dearborn, MI](/markets/detroit-warren-dearborn-mi)**
Median home prices remain under $250,000 in much of the metro, with a price-to-income ratio under 3x. The automotive industry's investment in EV manufacturing is creating high-wage jobs, and the city's downtown revitalization has real momentum. Detroit scores among the highest in the country on our affordability metrics.

**[Cleveland-Elyria, OH](/markets/cleveland-elyria-oh)**
One of the most affordable major metros in the country with a median price around $210,000. The healthcare sector (Cleveland Clinic, University Hospitals) provides a stable employment anchor. Buyers here face minimal competition, and the rent-to-price ratio is attractive for those considering house-hacking.

**[Pittsburgh, PA](/markets/pittsburgh-pa)**
The combination of major university systems (Carnegie Mellon, Pitt), a growing tech sector, and median prices under $230,000 makes Pittsburgh one of the best value propositions in the country. The market has been remarkably stable — less upside than Sun Belt markets, but far less risk.

### Post-Correction Markets with Improved Valuations

**[Austin, TX](/markets/austin-tx)**
Austin experienced one of the most significant corrections in the country, with prices declining 12-18% from the 2022 peak in many submarkets. For buyers who were priced out during the frenzy, 2026 offers a genuine second chance at a more rational price level. The tech employment base remains strong, and the quality-of-life factors that drove demand have not changed.

**[Phoenix-Mesa-Chandler, AZ](/markets/phoenix-mesa-chandler-az)**
Similar story to Austin: a meaningful correction has brought valuations back to earth after pandemic-era overheating. Inventory is plentiful by Phoenix standards, giving buyers leverage they have not had in years. The semiconductor manufacturing buildout (TSMC) provides a new demand driver beyond the traditional retiree and remote-worker pipeline.

**[Boise City-Nampa, ID](/markets/boise-city-nampa-id)**
Boise's correction was among the sharpest in the nation — prices fell roughly 15-20% from peak. For buyers willing to look past the post-boom hangover, the market's underlying appeal (outdoor lifestyle, growing tech presence, low taxes) remains intact at substantially better prices.

## Markets to Wait On

Not every market favors buyers right now. These metros remain challenging:

**[San Francisco-Oakland-Berkeley, CA](/markets/san-francisco-oakland-berkeley-ca)**
Despite meaningful price declines in the condo market, single-family homes in desirable neighborhoods remain profoundly expensive. The price-to-income ratio exceeds 9x in some submarkets. Tech layoffs have cooled demand but not enough to bring prices to accessible levels for most buyers. Unless you have substantial income or equity, the math still does not work for most households.

**[New York-Newark-Jersey City, NY-NJ](/markets/new-york-newark-jersey-city-ny)**
The New York metro remains one of the most expensive and competitive markets in the country. Inventory is chronically tight, particularly in Manhattan and the inner suburbs. Price-to-income ratios in desirable areas are extreme. For renters considering a purchase, the rent-vs-buy calculation still favors renting in many NYC neighborhoods when factoring in maintenance, taxes, and opportunity cost.

**[San Jose-Sunnyvale-Santa Clara, CA](/markets/san-jose-sunnyvale-santa-clara-ca)**
The heart of Silicon Valley continues to command premium prices that are disconnected from what most households can afford. Median home prices above $1.5 million mean that even high-earning tech workers face serious affordability constraints. Unless AI-driven valuations justify current pricing, this market carries elevated risk.

## How to Use Data to Decide

Asking "is it a good time to buy a house" in the abstract is like asking "is it a good time to eat" — the answer depends on whether you are hungry, what is available, and what you can afford. Here is how to use data to make the question concrete:

### Step 1: Know Your Market's Score
Visit PropertyIQ's [interactive map](/map) and look up the metro, county, or ZIP code where you are considering buying. Check the **HomeReady score** — a score above 65 indicates generally favorable buying conditions. Look at the **Market Health score** to understand whether the market is balanced, favoring buyers, or favoring sellers.

### Step 2: Check Affordability Metrics
On the market detail page, look at the price-to-income ratio and the estimated monthly payment relative to local median income. If the market's price-to-income ratio is below 4x, affordability is reasonable. Above 5x, you will need above-median income to buy comfortably.

### Step 3: Look at Inventory Trends
Months of supply tells you how much leverage you have as a buyer. Below 3 months means sellers still have the upper hand. Above 4 months means you can negotiate. Above 6 months means you have significant power to ask for concessions.

### Step 4: Consider the Trend, Not Just the Snapshot
Is inventory rising or falling? Are prices accelerating or decelerating? Are days on market increasing or decreasing? A market with a mediocre score *today* but rapidly improving trends may be a better bet than a market that scores well but is deteriorating.

### Step 5: Assess Your Personal Readiness
No amount of data can substitute for personal financial readiness. Do you have a stable income? An emergency fund beyond your down payment? Are you planning to stay for at least 5 years? If the answers are yes, a favorable market score is your green light. If the answers are uncertain, even the best market conditions will not protect you from the risk of buying before you are ready.

## The Bottom Line

**Is it a good time to buy a house in 2026?** In many markets, yes — conditions are more favorable for buyers than they have been in years. Rising inventory, fewer bidding wars, and stabilized mortgage rates have created opportunities that did not exist in 2021 or 2022.

But the answer is fundamentally local. A market in Detroit with a HomeReady score of 82 and 5 months of supply is a very different buying environment than San Francisco with a score of 41 and 1.8 months of supply. The data exists to answer this question with precision for *your* specific market. Use it.
